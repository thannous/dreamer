<h2 id="ancient-mesopotamia">Mesopotamia: the first written dreams</h2>
<p>The <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010124631" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Louvre dates Cylinder A of Gudea</a> to 2120–2110 BCE. Its Sumerian inscription tells how the god Ningirsu appeared to Gudea, ruler of Lagash, in a dream and asked him to build a temple. The dream is not a private puzzle here: it gives a royal project divine backing.</p>
<p>Later, scholars compiled lists of dream omens. A compendium from the archive of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal (7th century BCE) often works through wordplay, as <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://faculty.washington.edu/snoegel/PDFs/articles/Noegel%2045%20TGD%202006.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Scott Noegel's study</a> shows: eating a raven (<em>arbu</em>) meant income (<em>irbu</em>), because the words sound alike. The reading depended on language, not on how the dream felt. And none of this tells us who first interpreted a dream.</p>
<h2 id="ancient-egypt">Egypt: a manual of good and bad dreams</h2>
<p><a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10683-3" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Papyrus Chester Beatty III</a>, dated by the British Museum to around 1220 BCE, contains a dream book in hieratic script. Each entry gives a dream, a verdict, good or bad, then a meaning. Eating donkey meat was good: the dreamer would rise in rank. <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="teeth-falling-out-dreams">Teeth falling out</a>, still a common dream today, was bad: one of the dreamer's dependants would die.</p>
<p>Many of these links rest on puns that only work in Egyptian. The manual shows how a society reasoned about the night; it does not validate its predictions.</p>
<h2 id="ancient-greece">Greece: healing temples, a philosopher and a manual</h2>
<p>In sanctuaries of Asclepius such as Epidaurus, sick people <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.britannica.com/topic/Asclepius" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">slept in the temple</a> hoping the god would heal them in a dream. This is called incubation; some people now try a secular version by <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="dream-incubation-guide">asking a question before sleep</a>.</p>
<p>Aristotle takes another route. In <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/dreams.html" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>On Dreams</em></a>, dreams arise from traces of sensory impressions that persist once their object is gone. In <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/prophesying.html" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>On Prophesying by Dreams</em></a>, he notes that physicians watch dreams as possible signs of the body's state, but judges most “prophetic” dreams to be coincidences.</p>
<p>In the 2nd century CE, Artemidorus wrote the <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneirocritica" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>Oneirocritica</em></a>, a manual in five books. He insists that the interpreter know the dreamer: occupation, health, age, habits. The same image does not mean the same thing for everyone, an idea that has aged better than his predictions.</p>
<h2 id="religious-traditions">What do sacred texts say about dreams?</h2>
<p>In <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2041&amp;version=KJV" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Genesis 41</a>, Joseph reads Pharaoh's dreams of cattle and grain as seven years of plenty followed by seven of famine, an interpretation the text attributes to God. In <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%201%3A18-2%3A12&amp;version=KJV" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Matthew's Gospel</a>, another Joseph learns in a dream about Mary's <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="../symbols/pregnancy">pregnancy</a>.</p>
<p>The Talmud discusses dreams at length in tractate <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.sefaria.org/Berakhot.55a" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Berakhot</a>, where Rav Hisda compares an uninterpreted dream to an unread letter. In Islam, a <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6987" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari</a> calls a believer's good dream one of the forty-six parts of prophecy, and dream manuals circulated under the name of <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sirin" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Ibn Sirin</a> (died 728), an attribution some scholars doubt. The <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandukya_Upanishad" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Mandukya Upanishad</a> treats dreaming as a state of consciousness between waking and deep sleep, and Tibetan Buddhism developed dream yoga, close to <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="lucid-dreaming-beginners-guide">lucid dreaming</a>.</p>
<p>These passages say what their texts say. They are neither evidence that dreams <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="precognitive-dreams-science">predict the future</a> nor a summary of what every believer thinks.</p>
<h2 id="medieval-period">The Middle Ages: where do dreams come from?</h2>
<p>In the <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3095.htm#article6" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>Summa Theologiae</em> (II–II, question 95, article 6)</a>, Thomas Aquinas asks whether divination by dreams is unlawful. His answer depends on the cause. Some dreams come from waking thoughts or the state of the body, and can be read as signs, as a physician reads symptoms. Within his theology, others may come from God or from demons; seeking knowledge from demons is what he condemns. Natural and religious explanations sit side by side in one argument.</p>

<h2 id="freud-revolution">Freud: wishes and the unconscious</h2>
<p><a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66048" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>The Interpretation of Dreams</em></a> appeared in November 1899, dated 1900. Freud argues that a dream is the disguised fulfilment of a wish, and presents dream interpretation as a privileged route to unconscious mental life.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Manifest content:</strong> the dream as you remember and tell it.</li>
<li><strong>Latent thoughts:</strong> what Freud sought to reconstruct through analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Dream work:</strong> the transformations he proposed between the two, such as condensation and displacement.</li>
</ul>
<p>His method relied on free association: the dreamer says what each element brings to mind. The theory remains debated and is not a decoding manual. One part still serves anyone keeping a journal: your own associations count for more than a dictionary entry.</p>
<h2 id="jung-archetypes">Jung: dreams, myths and archetypes</h2>
<p>Carl Jung broke with Freud around the time of <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/65903/65903-h/65903-h.htm" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank"><em>Psychology of the Unconscious</em></a> (1912), which compares dream and fantasy material with mythology. He went on to describe a collective unconscious, recurring figures he called archetypes (the shadow, the anima and animus, the self), and dreams that compensate for a one-sided conscious attitude. These are interpretive concepts, not research findings: similar images in different stories do not prove that a symbol means the same thing for everyone.</p>
<h2 id="modern-neuroscience">Modern science: what can be measured?</h2>
<p>In 1953, <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13089671/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Aserinsky and Kleitman described recurring periods of rapid eye movement during sleep</a>, opening the lab study of <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="rem-sleep-dreams">REM sleep</a> and dreams. <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394322/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Siclari and colleagues (2017)</a> confirmed that people also report dreams after waking from non-REM sleep. Hobson and McCarley's <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.134.12.1335" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">activation-synthesis hypothesis</a> (1977), which sees dreams as the brain making sense of activity generated in REM sleep, was influential and remains debated.</p>
<h3>Dreams and waking life</h3>
<p>In a <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12590838/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">2003 diary study</a>, 29 participants logged their dreams and days for two weeks. About two thirds of the 299 reports (65%) contained elements of recent waking life, but only 1–2% replayed an actual episode. One study, not a universal rule, but it matches what journal keepers notice: dreams borrow from your days and rearrange them.</p>
<h3>Proposed functions are still hypotheses</h3>
<p><a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515147/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Revonsuo's threat-simulation hypothesis</a> suggests that dreaming rehearses threatening situations. It is a proposal, not an explanation of every <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="stop-nightmares-guide">nightmare</a>.</p>
<h3>Decoding and communicating with dreamers</h3>
<p>A Japanese team used brain imaging to <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://bicr.atr.jp/dni/en/research-projects/neural-decoding-of-visual-imagery-during-sleep/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">predict broad categories of images</a> reported by three participants at sleep onset (2013). In a <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">2021 study of 36 participants</a>, experimenters obtained 29 correct answers from six lucid dreamers during REM sleep. Limited demonstrations, not machines that read a dream.</p>

<h2 id="cultural-differences">How to read dream beliefs, and your own dreams</h2>
<p>One account cannot speak for a whole people. In the 17th century, Jesuit missionaries described Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) communities where a dream could express a wish of the soul that others helped fulfil; Anthony Wallace <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://dreamsofantiquity.ku.de/id/eprint/70186/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">analysed those outsiders' accounts in 1958</a>. Translation misleads too: the English word “Dreaming”, used for Aboriginal Australian concepts, <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="https://theconversation.com/dreamtime-and-the-dreaming-who-dreamed-up-these-terms-20835" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">refers to ancestral law and creation</a>, not mainly to nightly dreams.</p>
<p>A <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="../symbols/snake">snake</a>, <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="../symbols/water">water</a> or <a class="text-dream-salmon hover:underline" href="../symbols/fire">fire</a> can carry opposite meanings from one text to another. Before trusting an interpretation, ask who wrote it, when, and whether it describes a belief, a practice or a research finding.</p>
<h3>Three lenses on one dream</h3>
<p><strong>Fictional example</strong>, to show how this history can help you look at a dream without deciding its meaning for you:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dream:</strong> “My teeth came loose one by one while I was talking in a meeting.”</li>
<li><strong>Emotion:</strong> “Embarrassment more than pain. I tried to hide my mouth.”</li>
<li><strong>Recent context:</strong> “A job interview on Thursday.”</li>
<li><strong>Question to keep:</strong> “Does this dream come back before other moments when I am judged?”</li>
</ul>
<p>An Egyptian scribe would have seen a bad omen, Freud would have asked what teeth bring to mind, a researcher would note the recent worry. Only you can check which thread fits. One entry proves nothing; noting the same details over several nights is what makes patterns visible.</p>
</div>

<section class="mt-16">
<h2 class="font-serif text-2xl text-dream-cream mb-8">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<div class="space-y-4">
<details class="glass-panel rounded-xl p-4 group cursor-pointer">
<summary class="font-medium flex justify-between items-center text-dream-cream">Who first interpreted dreams?<i class="w-5 h-5 transition-transform group-open:rotate-180 text-dream-salmon" data-lucide="chevron-down"></i></summary>
<p class="mt-4 text-sm text-gray-400 leading-relaxed">Nobody can say. One of the oldest surviving examples is Cylinder A of Gudea, dated by the Louvre to 2120–2110 BCE, which tells of a dream about building a temple. It documents a practice, not its beginning.</p>
</details>
<details class="glass-panel rounded-xl p-4 group cursor-pointer">
<summary class="font-medium flex justify-between items-center text-dream-cream">Did Freud invent dream interpretation?<i class="w-5 h-5 transition-transform group-open:rotate-180 text-dream-salmon" data-lucide="chevron-down"></i></summary>
<p class="mt-4 text-sm text-gray-400 leading-relaxed">No. Aristotle and Artemidorus wrote about dreams more than 1,500 years before him. Freud proposed a psychological theory, published in 1899, built on the dreamer's own associations.</p>
</details>
<details class="glass-panel rounded-xl p-4 group cursor-pointer">
<summary class="font-medium flex justify-between items-center text-dream-cream">How do different cultures interpret dreams differently?<i class="w-5 h-5 transition-transform group-open:rotate-180 text-dream-salmon" data-lucide="chevron-down"></i></summary>
<p class="mt-4 text-sm text-gray-400 leading-relaxed">Dreams have been read as divine messages, omens, bodily signs or wishes of the soul. But one text never represents a whole community: ask who recorded an interpretation, when, and in what context.</p>
</details>
<details class="glass-panel rounded-xl p-4 group cursor-pointer">
<summary class="font-medium flex justify-between items-center text-dream-cream">What does modern science say about dream meaning?<i class="w-5 h-5 transition-transform group-open:rotate-180 text-dream-salmon" data-lucide="chevron-down"></i></summary>
<p class="mt-4 text-sm text-gray-400 leading-relaxed">Sleep labs measure brain activity and collect dream reports. Dreams often borrow from recent waking life, but research has found no universal code for symbols. Meaning remains a personal reading.</p>
</details>
